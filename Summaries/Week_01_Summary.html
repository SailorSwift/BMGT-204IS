If you remember one thing

Excel answered a question it had no business answering

We opened compactsuv.xlsx — 15 compact SUVs — and asked for the average owner satisfaction. That column holds +, ++, 0, and -. Here is what Excel said.

Owner Satisfaction · column E · rows 2–16
=AVERAGE(E2:E16)0
=COUNT(E2:E16)7
=COUNTA(E2:E16)15
No warning. No error. A clean number — built from 7 of the 15 cars, because seven of those cells hold the number zero and the other eight hold text, and AVERAGE discards text silently. The gap between COUNT and COUNTA is the only reason we know anything went wrong.

This course is not how do I compute things — Excel computes things. It is: what can I responsibly conclude from imperfect data?

The contrast worth knowing

In nations.xlsx, =AVERAGE(D2:D61) on Fitch Rating returns #DIV/0! — that column is entirely text, so Excel refuses out loud. Same illegal operation, two behaviours. The dangerous one is the one that doesn't error.

§1.2 · The four words

Every data set for the rest of this course looks like this

Rows are the things. Columns are what you measured about them. When a data set confuses you in November, the first question is always: what is one row?

Element
The thing being measured.
15 vehicles
Variable
A characteristic measured on each element.
6 variables
Observation
All the measurements for one element — one row.
15 observations
Data set
All of it together.
90 data values
15 elements × 6 variables = 90 data values

The six variables are Make, Overall Score, Recommended, Owner Satisfaction, Overall MPG, and Acceleration. Model is the row's label, not a variable — it names the element rather than measuring it. Make is a real variable, because it repeats: 13 distinct makes across 15 vehicles, with Volkswagen and Jeep appearing twice each.

We saw the same four words on a second file so they wouldn't get stuck to one table: nations.xlsx is 60 nations × 4 variables = 240 data values.

§1.2 · Scales of measurement

Four scales, and each one earns you one more operation

Read this as a ladder, not a list. Every rung keeps everything below it and adds exactly one thing you are newly allowed to do. That is why the indentation grows.

01 Categorical

Nominal

You can tell only that two things differ.

allows count · mode

Make · Recommended (7 Yes, 8 No) · WTO Status (57 Member, 3 Observer)

02 Categorical

Ordinal

Also which one is more.

adds median · ranks

Owner Satisfaction (- < 0 < + < ++) · Fitch Rating (16 grades, B− to AAA)

03 Quantitative

Interval

Also how much more — the gap is a fixed unit.

adds mean · differences

Overall Score — runs 50 to 84

04 Quantitative

Ratio

Also how many times more — zero means none.

adds ratios

Overall MPG (21–31) · Acceleration 0–60 (7.2–10.9 sec) · Per Capita GDP ($798 Rwanda to $87,097 Switzerland)

The mistake that costs the most points

“Categorical means text, quantitative means numbers” is wrong. Owner Satisfaction contains the number 0 and is ordinal. A zip code is a number and is nominal. A jersey number is a number and is nominal. It is never about what the cell contains — it is about what arithmetic on it would mean.

Left open on purpose

Two questions we did not settle

These are genuinely arguable, not answers being withheld. Both come back later in the course.

Q1

What is the average credit rating of these 60 nations?

Fitch Rating has 16 levels from B− to AAA. Ordinal data has a median, not a mean — the gap between AAA and AA+ is not the same size as the gap between B and B−, and you do not know what it is. Whether you may ever average an ordinal scale is a real argument among working researchers, not a settled rule. This course's position: you may, if you say out loud that you did it and why. Mean vs. median gets settled Sep 15 (§3.1).

Q2

Is a car scoring 84 twice as good as one scoring 42?

No — and that is exactly the interval/ratio line. Zero on Overall Score is a Consumer Reports convention, not an absence of quality, so the ratio of two scores means nothing. 31 MPG really is half again 21 MPG, because zero MPG is genuinely no miles per gallon.

§1.2 · Cross-sectional vs. time series

The same file can be either, depending on how you read it

Cross-sectional — many elements, one moment. compactsuv.xlsx is 15 vehicles from one model year. nations.xlsx is 60 nations measured at one point in time.

Time series — one thing, many time periods.

Then we opened gdp_years.xlsx — 30 countries × 6 years — and asked which it was. The honest answer: read a row and it's time series; read a column and it's cross-sectional. It is panel data, and it is both. The label matters less than knowing which direction your question runs.

A chart that is honest for one of these is misleading for the other — and we start making those charts on Sep 8.

§1.3 · Where data comes from

Four columns, four different kinds of knowing

§1.3 lists three ways to get data — existing sources, observational study, experiment. Most files illustrate one. compactsuv.xlsx contains all three side by side, formatted identically.

compactsuv.xlsx · provenance by column
Column How it was obtained Category
Overall MPG, Acceleration Consumer Reports drove the cars on a test track under controlled conditions Experiment
Owner Satisfaction They surveyed people who already own one Observational
Overall Score CR combined the measurements using weights of their own choosing Composite
Recommended A threshold applied to Overall Score Derived

The 0–60 time is a stopwatch on a test track — measure it again and you get 8.7 again. The satisfaction column is people's opinions, from whoever answered. And Recommended is not a measurement at all; it is a decision. Nothing about a spreadsheet tells you which is which. That is your job.

Recommended is manufactured, not measured

The lowest score marked Yes is 69. The highest marked No is 67. So Recommended is a threshold on Overall Score at roughly 68 — a categorical variable built by cutting a quantitative one. Who chose 68, and does it matter to the car at 57? The Chevrolet Equinox gets 31 MPG, the best on the list against a class average of 24 — and it is not recommended. You cannot tell why from this table.
