## 1. What today has to accomplish

This is the only session where you set expectations before anyone has a grade to
be anxious about. Everything else today is secondary to two things landing:

1. **Interpretation is the graded skill.** Excel does the arithmetic. Saying what it means is the course.
2. **The lowest quiz is dropped, and that *is* the make-up policy.** Every make-up request you field in November traces back to a student who did not hear this on August 27.

The content half of the period has a third job: convince a room that contains
several people who have already decided they are "bad at math" that this course is
about judgment, not arithmetic.

### Objectives

By the end of today a student can:

- **S1-1** State how the course is graded, what is due when, and what the AI policy permits.
- **S1-2** Give one concrete business decision improved by data, and one damaged by its misuse.
- **S1-3** Name three logical pitfalls and identify one in a claim put in front of them.

S1-1 is assessed by the absence of email in September. S1-2 and S1-3 appear on
Quiz 1 (question 4 is a claim containing a pitfall).

**Choose the chart in advance.** Slides 44–50 of `Chapters/CHAPTER3/Chap03_PPT.pptx`
are *Deceptive Graphs 1–7*. Slides 44, 45, 46, 48, and 50 contain a graphic; 47
and 49 are text-only, so they will not work as a silent hook.

I have not been able to see which of those five is the truncated-axis example —
open them and pick before class. What you want is the one where a small difference
looks enormous because the vertical axis does not start at zero. A misleading pie
or a dual-axis chart is a workable second choice.

**Run it like this.** Chart is up as they walk in. You say nothing about it for the
first five minutes — do the welcome over the top of it. Then:

> "Before anything else. What does this chart want you to believe? And what does

Take **three** answers. Do not take a fourth, do not confirm, do not explain the
trick. Then:

> "Hold onto that. We come back to this exact chart on September 8."

**Why leave it unresolved.** The eleven-day callback on Sep 8 is worth more than
any explanation you could give today, because on Sep 8 they will have the
vocabulary to name what they only sensed today. Resolving it now spends the asset.
Write "Aug 27 hook → resolve Sep 8" where you will see it.

## 5. Syllabus walkthrough (13–35 min, 22 minutes)

**Do not read the syllabus aloud.** Cover exactly these six items and point to the
document for everything else. Put the points table on screen for item 2.

**1. The shape of the week (2 min).** Tuesday introduces a method. Thursday opens
with a quiz, then practices it. Everything is due Tuesday; every quiz is Thursday.

> "You should never have to hunt through the syllabus to find out what's due."

**2. Grade weights (3 min).** The 1000-point table on screen. Emphasize quizzes
20% and the final exam 25%. Then say the thing they will not expect:

> "929 points is an A−. I don't round across a grade boundary. That's not me being
> harsh — it's me telling you in August instead of December."

**3. The quiz drop is the make-up policy (3 min).** Eleven quizzes given, best ten
count. State the consequence, not just the rule: no make-ups, no individually
excused quizzes. Then open the one door that matters:

> "If you already know you'll miss more than one Thursday — athletics, Guard
> drill, a conference — come see me **this week**, not the week it happens."

**4. Where the points actually are on projects (5 min).** Put the Small Project
rubric on screen: **30** correctness, **35** interpretation, **10**
reproducibility. Read the syllabus line out loud:

> *"A correct number with no business meaning attached earns partial credit at
> best."*

This is the single most useful sentence of the day. They will not believe it until
Small Project 1 comes back, so you will say it again on Sep 17 and Sep 22.

**5. The AI policy (5 min).** Permitted for learning; not permitted for producing
submitted work. Two conditions on every project: **disclosure** of what you used
it for, and **the explanation standard** — you can explain, in your own words and
on request, every number and every sentence you submit.

Then give the reason rather than only the rule:

> "This isn't suspicion. The skill this course certifies is looking at output and
> judging whether it's trustworthy. That is exactly the skill you lose by letting a
> model produce output you never examine. It's also the one that will still be
> worth something in your career."

And then, because it is what makes the policy real: **"I do ask."**

## 6. Core content (35–57 min)

### Why study statistics (35–45, slides 3–12)

Compress. **Pick two motivations, not six.** The deck interleaves *What is
Statistics?* (slides 4–7) with *Why Study Statistics?* (3, 8–12); you do not need
all of it.

The definition that sticks is not the textbook's:

> **Statistics is the science of turning data into a defensible claim.**

"Defensible" is the operative word, and it is worth one sentence of emphasis: the
whole course is about what makes a claim survive challenge.

**Draw the map of the semester on the board, once, by hand:**

```
        DESCRIBING                    CLAIMING
   what you actually have    →   something about what you don't
        Unit 1 (now)                Units 3–4 (October)
```

Students refer back to that drawing all term. Do it on the board rather than a
slide — the fact that you drew it makes it the room's, not the publisher's.

**Statistical challenges and imperfect data (slides 18–19)** deserve real time,
because they pre-empt a complaint you will otherwise hear all semester: *"this
dataset is messy."* Yes. That is the job.

### Statistics in business (45–57, slides 13–17 and 37–41)

Anchor on a business function students recognize — pricing, staffing, or quality —
rather than on statistics as a field.

**Then make it concrete with real data (3 min).** Open
`Chapters/CHAPTER1/Examples/Apple.xlsx`, **Data** sheet: Apple's revenue and
profit, 2015–2021, seven rows.

Do not analyze anything. Ask two questions:

1. *"If you're on Apple's board in 2021, what's the one question you'd ask of this table?"*
2. *"What would you need that isn't here to answer it?"*

The second question is the point. Seven rows of revenue and profit cannot tell you
*why*, and noticing what is absent is the analyst's instinct you are recruiting.

**Then plant a seed, in ten seconds.** Point at the sheet:

> "Notice the title is sitting in row 1 and the actual column headers are down in
> row 3. Every Excel tool we use assumes headers are in row 1. Remember that
> annoyance — September 3 is when we fix it."

That is a callback you will collect on Sep 3, and it costs you ten seconds now.

**Analytics in Action (slides 37–41)** — pick one, not four.

## 7. Logical pitfalls (57–68 min) — protect this block

Slides 26–32 (*Critical Thinking*, then *Logical Pitfalls 1–6*). **Run it as
think-pair-share, not lecture.** For each claim: 30 seconds silent, 60 seconds in
pairs, then take answers.

| Claim on screen | The pitfall | What to draw out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Ice cream sales and drowning deaths rise together, so ice cream is dangerous." | Correlation ≠ causation (confounder: summer) | Ask for the lurking variable **before** naming it |
| "Our site launched in March and Q2 sales rose 12%, so the site worked." | Post hoc ergo propter hoc | Ask: what else happened in Q2? |
| "I surveyed my 40 followers and 85% prefer our brand." | Unrepresentative sample | Plants Sep 1 |
| "Sales are up 300%!" (from 1 unit to 4) | Small base / missing denominator | Best one for a business audience |
| "This drug is 99% effective." (no control group) | Missing comparison | Introduces the baseline |

**Finish on the deck's own slide 36, *"Which Fallacy, if Any, do You See?"*** — the
"if any" is what makes it a good question, because it removes the guarantee that
something is wrong. That is the habit you want.

**Then the question that decides whether this room is with you:**

> "Which of these have you personally seen in an ad or a headline this month?"

Their examples are better than yours, and this is the moment the course becomes
about them rather than about a textbook. Budget 90 seconds and let it run if it is
working — this is the block to spend surplus time on.

## 10. Misconceptions to head off today

| --- | --- |
| *"Statistics is math."* | It uses arithmetic. The hard part is judgment. **Say this on day 1**, for the students who have already decided they are bad at this. |

| *"The syllabus is boilerplate."* | The rubric puts 35 of 75 points on interpretation. That is not boilerplate; that is the whole design. |
