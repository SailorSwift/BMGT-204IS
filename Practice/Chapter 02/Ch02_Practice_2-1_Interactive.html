Why bother with the second and third columns

19 is not an answer. 19 out of what?

A frequency tells you the size of a pile. A relative or percent frequency tells you the share — and share is the only one of the two that survives a change in sample size.

Suppose you run the same study at a second location and collect 40 purchases instead of 50.

Coca-Cola Store A  n = 50 Store B  n = 40 Larger
Frequency 19 16 Store A
Relative frequency 0.38 0.40 Store B
Percent frequency 38.0% 40.0% Store B
Same data, opposite conclusions. Read the frequency row and Store A looks better for Coca-Cola. Read either share row and it is Store B. Only the share rows are comparable, because only they account for the two different sample sizes.

This is why a count on its own cannot be compared to anything, and comparison is almost always the actual question — against another store, another year, another region, or a published market share.

Push on it

So which store is the larger market for soft drinks — and can you even tell?

No — and neither row gets you there. The numbers 50 and 40 are sample sizes. Somebody chose to observe 50 purchases at one store and 40 at the other. That choice says nothing about how much either store actually sells.

Share is a within-store quantity. It tells you how Coca-Cola performs against its shelf-mates at that location, and nothing at all about the size of the location. Store B could be three times the size of Store A and simply have been sampled less.

To get volume you would need a second variable entirely — total soft drink sales per store — and then multiply: total volume × share = Coca-Cola volume. A 40% share of a corner shop is fewer cans than a 38% share of a supermarket.

One more caution, which is Chapter 8 territory: 38% from 50 observations against 40% from 40 observations sits comfortably inside sampling noise. Samples this small cannot establish that Store B’s true share is higher at all. Shares fix the sample-size problem in the arithmetic; they do not make a small sample trustworthy.

The part that pays off in Chapter 4

Relative frequency is where probability quietly enters this course. When you divide 19 by 50, you have computed the empirical probability that a randomly chosen purchase is a Coca-Cola:

P(Coca-Cola) ≈ 19 / 50 = 0.38

Same arithmetic, different name. And it explains why the totals are worth checking: a relative frequency column summing to exactly 1.00 means the categories are non-overlapping and exhaustive — every observation landed in one class and only one. That is precisely the condition a probability distribution has to satisfy in Chapter 5. You will have built one by hand today without calling it that.

Why the total row matters

A frequency distribution has three invariants, and they are the only self-check you need: the frequencies sum to n = 50, the relative frequencies sum to 1.00, and the percent frequencies sum to 100. If a total is off, the mistake is almost always in the counting, not the arithmetic.

In Excel, get the counts with =COUNTIF($A$2:$A$51,"Coca-Cola") or a PivotTable, then divide by the total with an absolute reference — =B2/$B$7 — so the formula copies down without drifting.
