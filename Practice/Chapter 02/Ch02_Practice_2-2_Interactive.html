Why the class choice matters

The distribution you see is partly a decision you made

A categorical variable arrives with its classes already fixed — Coca-Cola is Coca-Cola. A quantitative variable does not. You choose the number of classes, and that choice changes the shape of the picture.

Run these same 20 audit times with 3 classes and you get a smooth mound that hides the long right tail. Run them with 15 classes and you get a scatter of ones and twos with no shape at all. Same data, three different stories — and only the middle one is informative.

That is why the textbook’s guidance is a range and not a formula: 5 classes for small data sets, up to 20 for large ones. Too few classes and you smooth away the detail you were looking for. Too many and every class holds one observation, which tells you nothing you could not read off the raw list.

The two hard rules are not negotiable, though. Classes must be non-overlapping — 10–15 and 15–20 is wrong, because an audit of exactly 15 days belongs to both. And they must be exhaustive, covering every observation from smallest to largest. Those two conditions are what make the frequencies sum to n, the relative frequencies sum to 1.00, and the whole thing a legitimate distribution — the same requirement you will meet again as a probability distribution in Chapter 5.

And it is why the width gets rounded up, never to the nearest value. Here 21 / 5 = 4.2, and rounding down to 4 gives five classes spanning only 20 days — one short of the 21-day range, leaving the largest audit with nowhere to go.

Doing this in Excel

Insert → PivotTable, drag Audit Time to both Rows and Values, then set the Values field to Count rather than Sum. Right-click any row label and choose Group to set the starting value, ending value, and interval — that is where your class width goes.

For the histogram, select a cell in the PivotTable, then Insert → Recommended Charts. Excel produces a bar chart with gaps; set the Gap Width to 0 in Format Data Series to make it a proper histogram.
