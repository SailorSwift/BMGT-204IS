Summarizing soft drink purchases in Excel

Seven steps from 50 rows of raw text to a finished frequency, relative frequency, and percent frequency distribution — plus the bar chart.

Before you start

Open softdrink.xlsx. You should see one column: the label Brand Purchased in cell A1, then 50 brand names in cells A2 through A51. Each row is one purchase — one observation of a single categorical variable.

There is nothing to compute yet. Excel cannot average a brand name. The only thing you can do with categorical data is count it — which is exactly what a frequency distribution is.

1

Insert the PivotTable

  1. Click any cell inside the data first — A1 works. This matters; see the note below.
  2. Click the Insert tab on the Ribbon.
  3. In the Tables group, click PivotTable.
  4. Check the dialog that appears, then click OK.
What the dialog is asking

Table/Range should read Sheet1!$A$1:$A$51. If this box is empty, Excel could not detect your data because the cursor was outside it — cancel, click A1, and start again. Always include row 1: the header becomes the field name, and leaving it out costs you an observation.

New Worksheet or Existing Worksheet. Either is fine. Choosing Existing Worksheet with a location of $C$1 keeps the summary beside the raw data, which is handy. The steps below assume you did that. A PivotTable grows down and to the right, so just do not drop it on top of column A.

Add this data to the Data Model. Leave it unchecked. That option is for relating several tables together, and ticking it makes grouping harder later.

2

Place the field twice

You get an empty PivotTable and a PivotTable Fields pane on the right listing one field: Brand Purchased. You need that same field in two places — once to make a row per brand, once to count them.

  1. Tick the checkbox beside Brand Purchased. Excel puts text fields into Rows automatically.
  2. Right-click Brand Purchased in the same list → Add to Values.

The counts appear immediately. Because the field holds text, Excel chooses Count on its own — summing brand names would be meaningless.
If the Fields pane keeps vanishing

The pane exists only while the selected cell is inside a PivotTable. A drag that starts a few pixels off the field name lands as a click on the worksheet, which deselects the PivotTable and the pane disappears. This is why the steps above use a checkbox and a right-click rather than dragging.

To bring it back: click any PivotTable cell. If it still does not appear, the toggle is off — PivotTable Analyze → Show → Field List. There is no setting that keeps it permanently visible; it is contextual by design.

3

Rename the headers, and find your rows

Click the Row Labels cell and type Brand. Click Count of Brand Purchased and type Frequency. The one rule: a new name cannot exactly match an existing field name, so Brand Purchased would be rejected.

Now note which rows your table actually occupies. Click the count next to Coca-Cola and read the Name Box at the top-left. Placed at C1, the headers land in row 1, the five brands in rows 2–6, and Grand Total in row 7. Every formula below uses those references — if your table sits elsewhere, adjust them to match.
What your sheet looks like, placed at C1
  C D E F
1 Brand Frequency Relative Frequency Percent Frequency
2 Coca-Cola 19 =D2/$D$7 =E2*100
3 Diet Coke 8 fill down fill down
4 Dr. Pepper 5 fill down fill down
5 Pepsi 13 fill down fill down
6 Sprite 5 fill down fill down
7 Grand Total 50 =SUM(E2:E6) =SUM(F2:F6)

Columns C and D belong to the PivotTable and are read-only — Excel refuses to let you type in them. Columns E and F are ordinary worksheet cells, and that is where all your formulas go. You are not replacing the 19; you are referring to it.

4

Relative frequency

Type the header in E1. In E2, divide Coca-Cola’s frequency by the total, then fill E2 down to E6.

=D2/$D$7

The dollar signs are the whole point of this step. Without them, filling down would slide the denominator too — row 3 would read =D3/D8, dividing by an empty cell. The $ locks D7 so every brand is divided by the same 50.
Type the references — do not click them

If you type =D2/ and then click cell D7 to pick it up, Excel will not insert D7. Because that cell belongs to a PivotTable it writes something like =GETPIVOTDATA("Brand Purchased",$C$1) instead, which will not fill down correctly.

Either type D2/$D$7 on the keyboard, or switch the behaviour off: PivotTable Analyze → Options dropdown → untick Generate GetPivotData. This is the single most common snag when building formulas beside a PivotTable, and it is not in the textbook.

5

Percent frequency

Header in F1. In F2, multiply the relative frequency by 100, then fill down to F6.

=E2*100

No dollar signs here — you want E2 to become E3, E4 and so on as you fill. Reach for $ only when a reference must stay put.
Alternative · let the PivotTable compute the percent

Right-click Brand Purchased in the field list → Add to Values a second time, then right-click any number in the new column → Show Values As → % of Grand Total. Rename the header to Percent Frequency.

This gives the percent column with no formula, but there is no equivalent for relative frequency — you still need step 4. Worth knowing: that column actually stores 0.38 and merely displays 38.00%. Percentage is a number format, not a different number. That is the same fact as =E2*100, seen from the other side.

6

Total the columns, then check them

In E7 enter the sum, then copy it across to F7.

=SUM(E2:E6)

Your three totals must read 50, 1.00, and 100. These are not decoration — they are the only built-in check this method has. A total that is off means a miscount upstream, not a rounding problem.
7

Bar chart

  1. Select any cell in the PivotTable.
  2. Click the Insert tab on the Ribbon.
  3. In the Charts group, click Recommended Charts. A preview of the bar chart appears.
  4. Click OK.

Then title the chart and label both axes. An untitled chart with bare axes is not finished work — a reader should understand it without being told what it shows. Keep the gaps between bars: these are five separate categories, not intervals on a scale. (In 2.2 you will close those gaps deliberately, and that is what makes a histogram a histogram.)
Where this usually goes wrong

Trying to type a formula into the PivotTable. Columns C and D are read-only; Excel will refuse with a message about affecting a PivotTable. Formulas go in E and F and refer to D2 and D7.

Using the textbook’s row numbers instead of your own. The book’s example prints =B4/$B$9 because its PivotTable starts lower down. Yours depends entirely on where you placed it. Read your own Grand Total row off the sheet before writing any formula.

Forgetting the dollar signs. If your relative frequencies come out as #DIV/0! errors or a column that will not total 1, the denominator drifted as you filled down.

Percent formatting on top of multiplying by 100. Apply Excel’s Percent format to a column that already holds 38 and you will see 3800%. Pick one; the textbook multiplies.

Treating Diet Coke as Coca-Cola. They are separate categories in this data set. Combine them only when a question asks about Coca-Cola products.
