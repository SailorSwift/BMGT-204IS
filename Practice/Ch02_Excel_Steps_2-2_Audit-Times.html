Summarizing audit times in Excel

Same PivotTable, two new problems: the data are numbers, so Excel wants to add them up — and the classes do not exist until you create them.

Before you start

Open audit.xlsx. Cell A1 holds the label Audit Time, and cells A2 through A21 hold 20 observations — the number of days required to complete a year-end audit for each of 20 clients.

If you did Activity 2.1, most of this will feel familiar. Two things are genuinely different, and both come from the data being quantitative rather than categorical. They are steps 1 and 4 below.

What changes when the data are numbers

Excel will try to add them. Drop a text field into Values and Excel counts it, because summing brand names is meaningless. Drop a number field in and it sums — giving you 366 total days, which answers nothing. You have to change it to Count yourself.

There are no categories to count. Coca-Cola was a category; 18 days is not. Without grouping you get one row per distinct value — a list almost as long as the raw data. You have to define the classes.

1

Work out the classes first — on paper

Do this before touching a menu, because step 4 will ask you for the numbers.

  1. Choose the number of classes. Five to twenty, depending on sample size. With n = 20, use 5.
  2. Find the range. Largest 33, smallest 12, so the range is 21.
  3. Divide. 21 ÷ 5 = 4.2.
  4. Round up to a convenient value: a class width of 5.
  5. Set the limits from a round number at or below the smallest value: 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34.

Round up, never to the nearest. Rounding 4.2 down to 4 gives five classes covering only 20 days, one short of the 21-day range — and the 33-day audit would have nowhere to go.
2

Insert the PivotTable

  1. Click any cell inside the data first — A1 works.
  2. Insert tab → Tables group → PivotTable.
  3. Confirm Table/Range reads Sheet1!$A$1:$A$21. Leave Add this data to the Data Model unchecked.
  4. Choose Existing Worksheet, location $C$1, then OK.

An empty Table/Range box means Excel could not detect your data because the cursor was outside it. Cancel, click A1, try again. Ticking the Data Model box will block the grouping you need in step 4, so leave it alone.
3

Place the field, then change Sum to Count

  1. Tick the checkbox beside Audit Time in the Fields pane.
  2. Right-click Audit Time → Add to Values.
  3. The Values box now reads Sum of Audit Time. Click it → Value Field Settings.
  4. Under Summarize value field by, choose Count. Click OK.

You now have a long list — one row per distinct audit time, each with a count of 1 or 2, and a Grand Total of 20. That is correct but useless, and step 4 fixes it.

If the Fields pane keeps disappearing, that is normal — it only exists while the selected cell is inside the PivotTable. Click any PivotTable cell to bring it back, or use PivotTable Analyze → Show → Field List.
4

Group the values into your classes

This is the step that has no counterpart in 2.1, and it is where your step 1 numbers go.

  1. Right-click any row label in the PivotTable (any audit time value) → Group.
  2. Starting at: 10
  3. Ending at: 34
  4. By: 5
  5. Click OK.

The long list collapses to five rows: 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, with frequencies 4, 8, 5, 2, 1 and a Grand Total of 20.

Excel pre-fills Starting at with 12 and Ending at with 33 — your actual minimum and maximum. Overwrite both. Accepting 12 gives classes of 12–16, 17–21 and so on: arithmetically valid, but not the round limits the textbook uses, and much harder to read.

Columns C and D belong to the PivotTable and are read-only. Columns E through I are ordinary worksheet cells — that is where every formula goes. Check your own row numbers before typing: these assume the table was placed at C1.

5

Relative and percent frequency

In E2 and F2, then fill both down to row 6:

=D2/$D$7
=E2*100

Type the references rather than clicking the cells. Clicking a PivotTable cell mid-formula makes Excel write GETPIVOTDATA(...), which will not fill down. Or switch it off first: PivotTable Analyze → Options dropdown → untick Generate GetPivotData.
6

The three cumulative distributions

A cumulative frequency counts everything at or below a class’s upper limit. In G2:

=SUM($D$2:D2)

Only the start of the range is anchored. That is the trick worth learning. Fill down and the range grows: row 3 becomes SUM($D$2:D3), row 4 becomes SUM($D$2:D4). The window keeps its left edge and stretches its right, which is exactly what “everything so far” means.

Then H2 is =G2/$D$7 and I2 is =H2*100. Fill all three down to row 6.

You should get 4, 12, 17, 19, 20 and 20%, 60%, 85%, 95%, 100%. Do not total these columns — they are already running totals, and the last row must equal n by definition.
7

Histogram — and close the gaps

  1. Select any cell in the PivotTable.
  2. Insert tab → Charts group → Recommended Charts → OK.
  3. Right-click any bar → Format Data Series.
  4. Set Gap Width to 0%.

Step 4 is not cosmetic — it is what makes the chart a histogram. In 2.1 the gaps between bars were correct, because Coca-Cola and Sprite are separate categories with nothing between them. Here the classes are adjacent intervals on a continuous scale: 14 days and 15 days sit next to each other with no space in between, so the rectangles must touch.

Finish by adding a chart title and both axis titles — Audit time (days) across, Frequency up.
Where this usually goes wrong

Leaving it on Sum. If your Grand Total reads 366 instead of 20, Excel is adding the audit times rather than counting them. Value Field Settings → Count.

Accepting Excel’s grouping defaults. It offers 12 and 33 because those are your min and max. Type 10 and 34 instead, or you get classes nobody can read at a glance.

No Group option in the right-click menu. Either you right-clicked a value rather than a row label, or the field is text. Numbers stored as text cannot be grouped — check that the audit times are right-aligned in column A.

Anchoring both ends of the cumulative range. =SUM($D$2:$D$2) gives the same 4 in every row. Only the first reference gets dollar signs.

Rounding the class width down. 4.2 rounds up to 5, never down to 4. Round down and your classes do not span the data.
